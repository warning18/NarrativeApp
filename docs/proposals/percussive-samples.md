## 470, the cloth under the trapdoor (the longest scene)

### Before, EN (1573 characters)

Whichever way I had come, I came back to what was left of our house in the grey before dawn, when the street held nothing but smoke. The searchers had gone. They had pried up every board in the place and found nothing under any of them, because what they wanted had never been under a board.

The cannonball had found it for them, and they had not stayed to see: it had gone on down through the floor of the back room and a hand’s depth of packed earth beneath, and laid bare **a square of old cedar with an iron ring set in it**. A trapdoor. I had lived over it my whole life.

Under it, in a hole lined with oilcloth, lay a single folded piece of cloth, grey with age, and when I lifted it out it unfolded, and kept unfolding, until it hung from my hands to the floor: **a cloth as big as a cape**. That was all there was. A family’s hidden treasure, the thing a city had been torn apart for, and it was a piece of old cloth.

Who could want such an old piece of cloth? Then the light came up, and I saw what was on it. A figure, very large, larger than the cloth had any business holding, standing with **both arms raised over its head and both hands open, palms out**, the way a child draws someone waving from a long way off.

Nobody had stitched it. Nobody had painted it. It was simply there, the way a bruise is there, and it was warm under my thumb. In my pocket, the old bone die had gone warm as well. I put the cloth, the die, and what the searchers had missed into one bundle, and found I knew exactly what I was carrying, and how little room there was for it.

## 260, the toll bridge (a street scene)

### Before, EN (602 characters)

The bridge spanned a river in a genuine hurry to be elsewhere. A handful of slum toughs had claimed it in the Inquisition’s name: white armbands knotted on over their own colours with more enthusiasm than conviction, and weapons held ready in the manner of men who have recently discovered how easy killing is and are looking for an excuse to confirm it.

A toll booth, hammered together out of somebody’s market stall, sat astride the only way across. The man inside it had a ledger, a quill and a club, and appeared to consider all three official.

"**Toll**," he said. "For the Inquisition’s peace."

## 800, the Black Hold

### Before, EN (1443 characters)

Captured, then. I woke in the Black Hold, which is a name I did not choose and cannot in good conscience improve upon. **They told me Lysa was dead**. For three days a man in a porcelain mask, whom the guards called Brother Clement, took me apart with the patient, faintly bored thoroughness of someone doing accounts he would rather not be doing.

He wanted to know about a cloth. The cloth my mother kept, he said, as if I ought to know which: how long it was, what was drawn on it, where she had hidden it, who else had seen it; and most of all the hands — **how many fingers, and whether they were open or shut**.

I had never seen any such cloth. I told him so, which cost me considerably, chiefly because it happened to be the truth, and the truth, I have since learned, satisfies an interrogator far less than a good lie. **On the fourth day** the world exploded, which I took, under the circumstances, as an improvement.

The wall went in a cloud of brick-dust and fire: resistance fighters had breached the perimeter, men and women I did not know and did not, in the moment, need to.

One of my torturer’s assistants stumbled past me with a fist-sized piece of masonry lodged in his neck, wearing an expression of such profound professional surprise that I found, despite everything, something close to satisfaction in it.

I was unshackled, stripped to my shirt, and free, and you can build quite a lot on that particular foundation.

## 891, the high quay

### Before, EN (1022 characters)

The high quay stood over the harbor on stone legs, and along it, moored by their keels to iron posts as thick as a man, rode the vessels that should not have floated. I had seen them all my life and never once believed in them: long hulls with no water under them, swaying at their ropes a man’s height above the stones, their sails hanging slack and painted from yard to foot with signs.

The crews were wetting the sails with buckets as I came up the steps, and where the water ran, the signs shone like something lit from inside; on the nearest, a woman on a plank was going over one of them with a brush, carefully, the way you would retouch a face.

Half the city was trying to get aboard. The first vessel to cast off, **a narrow grey-hulled flier called the Lark**, had begun to ring her bell, and a mate with a manifest stood at her gangway counting heads. The armory under the quay had been looted to the walls, the shieldwright’s cart lay on its side, and **there was time to grab one thing, and not much else**.

## 1150_vica, the dog in the dunes

### Before, EN (1137 characters)

I left the Face with the first stars, and had gone perhaps two hundred paces when the dunes closed behind me like a door. The wind had already taken my tracks.

I do not know how long. The sun was a white coin, then it was not there. Every dune was the same dune and my mouth was sand. I sat down, because walking in circles cost more than sitting.

Something moved at the edge of my eye: **a dog**, small and square, white with black patches under the dust, flat-faced, her ears standing up like a bat’s. She looked at me the way you look at a decision. She turned, took three steps, and looked back.

I followed because I had nothing left to argue with. She stopped at every crest and waited for me, and she did not once look at the sky.

The lights came up out of the dark all at once: a hollow in the dunes, palms, and a scatter of white clay huts glowing like loaves in an oven. She sat down at the edge of the light and would not go any further without me.

I called her **Vica**. I could not have told you why; the word came up out of the heat like the name of an old friend, and she turned her head as if it had been said before.

## 1200, the White Wells

### Before, EN (1285 characters)

The lights were an oasis. I had heard the word in stories and never expected to need it: a green pool in a hollow of the dunes, palms leaning over it as if to drink, and around it a scatter of huts built of white clay, square and low, glowing in the dark like loaves in an oven.

The White Wells, the people there called it, as if there were nowhere else to be. A salt caravan was camped by the water with its camels kneeling, and three or four merchants were rolling up their mats for the night. Nobody asked where I had come from. Everybody there had come from somewhere.

An old woman with the salt-cutters, who had been watching me, came and sat beside me at the water and looked at the corner of grey cloth showing at the top of my pack, and went still, and reached over without a word and tucked it out of sight.

"**Keep that covered**," she said. "Here, and wherever you go. There are people who would cut it off your back, and people who would cut you off it." Then, because I asked, she told me where a person with no ship might find one: south, **ten days with the caravan**, where the Waste comes down to the sea at a city called Saltmouth.

They broke ships there, she said, and built them, and sold them to anyone with coin and no questions. And then she told me to rest.

Il y a des gens qui vous le couperaient sur le dos, et des gens qui vous couperaient, vous, pour l’avoir. » Puis, parce que je le lui demandai, elle me dit où quelqu’un sans navire pouvait en trouver un : au sud, **dix jours de caravane**, là où la Désolation descend jusqu’à la mer, dans une ville nommée Bouche-de-Sel.

On y démolissait des navires, dit-elle, on en construisait aussi, et on les vendait à quiconque avait de l’argent et pas de questions. Et puis elle me dit de me reposer.
